Like many others (though not all), I look forward every year to seasonal Christmas music. Uplifting lyrics about fun, family, and goodwill towards men; classic hymns; jazz; a cappella vocal harmony—some of my favorite things! But, also like many others, it is sometimes hard to tell when it is appropriate to make the transition, with many marking it far too early. What is a pious Orthodox Christian to do?

![A smiling woman in a Santa outfit peeks out from behind a decorated Christmas tree in a snowy Red Square at sunset, with St. Basil’s Cathedral in the background.](/blog/when-is-it-ok-to-start-playing-christmas/cover.jpg)

On the one hand, in the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Nativity Fast, a period of 40 days leading up to Christmas Day (analogous to the Western Advent), begins on November 15. So it might seem that this is the official beginning of the “Christmas season” and an acceptable starting date.

On the other hand, however, it is also true that this is a period of fasting rather than festival, and that there is nothing really done liturgically to mark this point. So that clearly counts against such an early date.

But it should be noted that just five days after the beginning of the fast, on the eve of the feast of the Presentation of the Theotokos (Nov. 21) the church first sings the *katavasia* of the Christmas matins canon that doubles as the Nativity greeting: “Christ is born! Glorify Him!” This is then repeated at all festal and Sunday matins for the duration of the season.

*Therefore, I conclude that it is permissible to enjoy all Christmas music beginning the evening of November 20*

This is the ruling according to *economia*, and I think it should hold for all Orthodox Christians living where Christmas is a major cultural holiday. For those ascetics who wish to keep the fast with greater *akrivia*, I offer two further rules:

- Our fasting calendar already distinguishes between days in which a strict fast is called for and days when wine, oil, and fish are allowed. So we can distinguish between different types of Christmas music. Liturgical hymnody and traditional carols are like water, allowed every day. Secular standards are like wine and oil, reserved for weekends or special feasts. “All I Want for Christmas is You” and Wham!’s “Last Christmas” perhaps are meat and dairy, forbidden until Christmas Day itself.
- Songs about Santa Claus (i.e., St. Nicholas) are only allowed beginning on his feast day, December 6.

Note that the discussion here presumes the New Calendar dates. “Christ is Born!” is first sung on the Old Calendar on the evening of December 3. Those on the Old Calendar should discuss with their spiritual father whether their *economia* extends to the earlier date. Those sticking with the later date can at least console themselves with the fact that they can further live out their counter-cultural ethos by continuing to blast Christmas music well into the middle of January.
